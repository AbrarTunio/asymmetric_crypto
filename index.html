What is asymmetric (public-key) cryptography?

Cryptography protects messages from people who shouldn't read them or change them. It comes in two big families, and today's activity is about the second one.

🔑 Symmetric: one shared key

The same key locks and unlocks, like a house key. It's very fast (AES protects your Wi-Fi and stored files).

The problem: both people must already have the same key. How do you hand it to someone across the internet without an eavesdropper copying it?

vs
🔐 Asymmetric: a pair of keys

Everyone has a public key and a private key. Think of the public key as an open padlock you give to anyone, and the private key as the only key that opens it.

Anyone can lock a box for you. Only you can open it. No secret ever has to be shared first.

What are we learning in Mission 1?

What is Diffie–Hellman key exchange?

A method that lets two people who have never met agree on the same secret key while talking over a channel that anyone can listen to. The secret key itself is never sent: each side builds it from its own private secret plus the other side's public value.

🧠 In plain words

Start from a shared public value, mix in your own secret, swap the results in public, then mix in your secret again. Both sides land on the same answer. The eavesdropper sees everything that was sent but can't “un-mix” it.

🌍 Where you meet it every day
  • Every HTTPS website (TLS uses a modern elliptic-curve version)
  • WhatsApp and Signal chats
  • VPNs such as WireGuard and IPsec
  • SSH logins to servers
🎯 What you'll do in this mission

Mix paint, run the real maths with small numbers, play the eavesdropper trying to crack it, and watch how an attacker in the middle can fool it.

📜 Published in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman: the first public description of public-key cryptography.⚠️ It only agrees a key. On its own it doesn't prove who you are talking to.
Key facts and learning outcomes read together, about 5 minutes
🧩 The problem

Symmetric ciphers like AES need both sides to already share a key. How can two strangers agree one over a network that Ehsan can read?

🗝️ Key pairs

Each person holds a public key anyone may see and a private key never shared. What one key does, only the other can undo or check.

🤝 Diffie–Hellman

Public numbers p and g. Ayesha keeps a secret a, Bilal keeps b. They swap A = ga mod p and B = gb mod p, and both end with the same s.

🕵️ Why Ehsan fails

Getting a back from A is the discrete logarithm problem: easy forwards, infeasible backwards. But plain DH can't tell who sent A, so a man-in-the-middle can swap it.

RSA: construction, use and pitfalls

0 of 3 tasks done
🔐
What are we learning in Mission 2?

What is RSA?

A public-key system that can encrypt (anyone locks a message with your public key; only your private key unlocks it) and sign. Its keys are built from two secret prime numbers.

🧠 In plain words

Multiplying two primes is easy. Splitting the answer back into those two primes is incredibly hard when the numbers are hundreds of digits long. RSA's private key hides behind that difficulty.

🌍 Where you meet it every day
  • The certificate behind your browser's padlock
  • Signing software and app updates
  • Secure email (S/MIME, PGP)
  • Smart cards and banking systems
🎯 What you'll do in this mission

Build a real (tiny) RSA key pair from two primes, lock and send a secret number, then judge real-world mistakes that break RSA.

📜 Invented in 1977 by Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman at MIT. “RSA” is their initials.💡 RSA is slow, so in practice it protects small things like keys, and AES encrypts the actual data.
Key facts and learning outcomes read together, about 5 minutes
⚒️ Making keys

Pick primes p, q. Then n = p × q and φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1). Choose e sharing no factor with φ. Find d so that e × d leaves remainder 1 when divided by φ.

📬 Using them

Public key (e, n), private key (d, n). To send Bilal a secret, lock it with Bilal's public key; only his private key opens it.

🧱 Why it's secure

To find d you need φ(n), which needs p and q. Getting those from n means factoring it: easy for small n, impossible for a 2048-bit n.

🕳️ Pitfalls

“Textbook” RSA is predictable. Always use padding: OAEP to encrypt, PSS to sign. Avoid small keys and weak or related primes.

lock: c = me mod n     unlock: m = cd mod n

✍️
What are we learning in Mission 3?

What is a digital signature?

The digital version of a handwritten signature plus a tamper-proof seal. The sender signs with their private key; anyone can check it with the sender's public key. Change even one character and the check fails.

🧠 In plain words

Signing means transforming the message's fingerprint (its hash) with your private key. Only you can make it; everyone can check it. It gives three guarantees: who sent it, unchanged since signing, and the sender can't deny it later.

🌍 Where you meet it every day
  • HTTPS certificates (signed by trusted authorities)
  • App store and Windows/Android updates
  • E-signed PDFs and contracts
  • Cryptocurrency transactions (Bitcoin uses ECDSA)
🎯 What you'll do in this mission

Sign a message and catch a forgery, decide when a MAC is enough, draw point addition on an elliptic curve, and check the standards: DSS and ElGamal.

📜 RSA signatures (1977), ElGamal signatures (1985), NIST's Digital Signature Standard (1994, now FIPS 186-5). Elliptic-curve cryptography was proposed by Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller in the mid-1980s.
Key facts and learning outcomes read together, about 5 minutes
✍️ Signing

Ayesha takes the message's fingerprint (hash) and transforms it with her private key. Anyone checks it with her public key. It proves integrity, who sent it, and non-repudiation.

🔑 Why a MAC can't do this

A MAC uses a shared key, so Bilal could have made the tag himself. It can't convince an outsider, so there's no non-repudiation.

📜 DSS (FIPS 186)

NIST's Digital Signature Standard. FIPS 186-5 (2023) approves RSA, ECDSA and EdDSA; the original DSA is kept only for checking older signatures.

🎲 ElGamal

A discrete-log signature (DSA and ECDSA descend from it). Every signature uses a fresh random k. Reuse k once and the private key can be worked out.

🌀 Elliptic curves

Points on a curve can be “added” with a line-and-reflect rule. Security rests on the ECDLP. A 256-bit curve key is about as strong as 3072-bit RSA.
